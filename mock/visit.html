3 · Covered Bridges of Windsor County

Fifteen covered bridges, a short drive apart

One of Vermont's most unique and beloved sights. Windsor County alone is home to fifteen. This list is still growing.

Quechee Covered Bridge

Town: Quechee
River: Ottauquechee
Address: Waterman Hill Rd #4, Quechee, VT 05059 (Google Maps)

A modern steel bridge with a traditional wood covering, originally constructed in the 1970s and rebuilt after Hurricane Irene in 2013. It passes over the Ottauquechee River waterfall and is popular with hot-air balloon watchers at the Quechee Balloon Festival, visitors to the nearby Simon Pearce Restaurant, and sightseers.

Taftsville Covered Bridge

Town: Woodstock
River: Ottauquechee
Address: 403 E Woodstock Rd, Woodstock, VT 05091 (Google Maps)

Originally built in 1836, the Taftsville Covered Bridge measures 186 feet and reflects an earlier, "craftsman" bridge-building style, with wood-and-steel construction. It is among the longest of Vermont's covered bridges and popular with sightseers along the Ottauquechee River.

Lincoln Covered Bridge

Town: Woodstock
River: Ottauquechee
Address: 530 US-4, Woodstock, VT 05091 (Google Maps)

Originally built in 1877, the Lincoln Covered Bridge is a registered US historic site, as one of the only remaining examples of its style. It stands 136 feet in length, with wood-and-steel construction, and is popular with locals and visitors alike.

Middle Covered Bridge

Town: Woodstock
River: Ottauquechee
Address: Mountain Ave, Woodstock, VT 05091 (Google Maps)

Built in 1877 originally as an iron bridge and replaced in 1969 in the form you see today, the Middle Covered Bridge (sometimes called the "Union Street Bridge") is built in a beautiful wood-only lattice-and-peg method. It caught fire in 1974 (on the night of the Firemen's Ball, no less!) but suffered only minimal damage and was refurbished.

History of Vermont's covered bridges

Vermont is nationally famous for its more than 100 authentic covered bridges, the third most in the United States and the most in New England, popular with locals and visitors alike.

From the early days of European settlement, covered bridges kept farmers' sheep and cattle dry and sheltered on the way to market. Today they carry single-lane traffic and draw sightseers. The Vermont Covered Bridge Society provides maps, tours and contests to help visitors appreciate them and keep them in good condition.

4 · Area History and Information

Hartford's five villages: history and attractions

Each village has its own history and charm, from the industries of the 1800s to Revolutionary War-era farmsteads.

Hartford Village

A historic hub on the White River

Located along the White River, Hartford Village boasts a diverse industrial past, including the production of chairs, tools, lumber, carriages and textiles. One of its notable historical figures is Horace Wells, the first dentist to use "laughing gas" as anesthesia.

Today it is a vibrant, walkable community with excellent fishing spots, swimming holes and a boat launch at Watson Memorial Park. The southern end of the park also features the Upper Valley's only dog park.

Quechee

From mill town to resort destination

Settled in the 1760s, Quechee's economy revolved around Ottauquechee River mills producing lumber, cider and fabric. It became known for "shoddy" fabric at J.C. Parker and Co. and for baseball uniforms for the Boston Red Sox and New York Yankees at Dewey and Company.

After a mid-1900s decline, the 1960s brought a revival with the Quechee Lakes Resort, a four-season community. Quechee Gorge, "Vermont's Little Grand Canyon," attracts over 200,000 visitors annually for hiking, fishing and winter sports.

White River Junction

From railroad hub to arts district

Its location once made White River Junction one of New England's most important railroad hubs, a center for manufacturing and warehousing. The interstate in the 1960s shifted commerce away from downtown and led to a period of decline.

It has since been revitalized as a hub for arts and culture, with galleries, professional theater, music venues and restaurants anchoring the Upper Valley's creative economy.

West Hartford

Rural charm along the White River

The most rural of the five villages, West Hartford offers whitewater kayaking, canoeing, tubing, fishing and swimming, with open fields and scenic views of the White River Valley.

It has a historic country store and public library. A focal point is the new Patriot's Bridge honoring Hartford's service members, beside a pocket park dedicated to local heroes.

Wilder

A planned community with historic roots

Originally known as Olcott Falls, Wilder was named after Charles Wilder, who established the massive International Paper Company plant producing newspaper and pulp. Today it is home to the Wilder Dam and Salmon Ladder, part of TransCanada's hydroelectric system on the Connecticut River.

It is a popular residential community near employment centers in Hanover, with well-planned neighborhoods and two public parks.
